### The promise

Every refusal `/api/verify` can produce ended with "email support and we will
unlock it by hand." I wrote that sentence, deployed it, and put it in the
support runbook, and at no point did I ask what the person reading the email
would actually *do*. The answer is nothing. A licence is a record in the
buyer's own browser holding an email and a token; nothing validates the token.
There is no account to flip, no row to write, no licence to issue. The most
important promise on the site — the one that catches a customer who has already
paid $19 and been told no — had no mechanism behind it at all.

Two things fixed it, and the order matters.

**`scripts/unlock.mjs`** (new, `npm run unlock`). Read-only, GETs to Stripe
only. Takes an email, a name, a card's last four, a `cs_live_` session id off
the receipt, or `--recent`. For each session it prints when, how much, paid or
not, the name and email Stripe actually holds, livemode — and then the thing
that matters: **whether `/api/verify` will find it**, computed the same way the
Worker computes it. Then a paste-ready reply. `test/supportlookup.mjs` (new, 21
checks) drives it against a fake Stripe through every branch: findable, paid
with no email on file, never paid, nothing found — and asserts it never sends
anything but a GET.

**The wording.** Both 404 messages now end "we will find it and sort it out by
hand", because that is what the tool above does and the other sentence was a
lie. `support.md` got the procedure, and the line in it that still described
the old wording is gone.

The lesson is not "write a tool." It is that I shipped a promise whose
implementation I had never once pictured, and it survived a full test suite, a
live deploy, and being written into a runbook — because nobody had triggered it
yet. Nothing tests a sentence.

## Phase 21 — the cold visitor (02:42–03:5x)

I wrote two scripts under a rule: they may not use a single selector I know
from having built the app. They find things by geometry and visible text, the
way a stranger does. The point was to answer a question no suite in this repo
asks — not "does the product work" but "can somebody who arrived from a
Product Hunt link get to a finished book without help."

**The good news first, because most of it was good news.**

Landing to a downloaded PDF, accepting every default and pressing only the most
obvious button: **4.4s on desktop Chromium, 7.6s on a WebKit iPhone 13**, a
real 660+ KB book both times, zero page errors. The fold on a 664px phone holds
the headline, the lede, both buttons, the sample links, and — at y=532, above
the fold — "Free: a full-length book, watermarked. $19 once removes the mark."
No horizontal overflow anywhere.

**The one real defect: nothing on the first screen was a thumb target.**

The five hero sample links were **18px** tall. The "Pricing" link in the header
— the only nav link a phone shows, sitting in the top-right corner, the hardest
place on a phone to hit — was **22px**. Apple's number is 44.

That matters more than the size suggests. A stranger who has never heard of
this opens a sample *before* trying anything; it is the cheapest way to find
out whether the thing is real. Five 18px links 3px apart means a thumb lands on
the neighbour, and "I tapped sudoku and got a maze" is indistinguishable from
"this is broken."

The fix is two CSS rules, both inside `@media (pointer: coarse)`, both using
padding cancelled by a matching negative margin:

```css
@media (pointer: coarse) {
  header a { display: inline-block; padding-block: 11px; margin-block: -11px; }
  .hero-cta .note a { display: inline-block; padding-block: 13px; margin-block: -13px; }
}
```

So: the target grows, the text does not move, the layout does not reflow, and
a desktop visitor gets **byte-identical rendering** — `pointer` describes the
*primary* input, so even a touchscreen laptop with a trackpad reports `fine`
and never sees these rules. That is what made it safe to ship on launch eve.
Measured after: every control above the fold is 44–49px on iPhone 13 and
45–49px on Pixel 7, and the desktop probe is unchanged at 18/23px.

**Why the existing mobile suite missed it.** `test/mobile.mjs` prints "controls
are thumb-sized" and passed the whole time. It checks controls matching
`#tool button, #tool select, ...` — the tool's own controls — at a **36px**
bar, while its comment claimed 44. So it was green, honest about nothing, and
saying a sentence broader than what it measured. Two fixes: the success line
now says *which* controls it checked and points at the new suite, and the
comment now says 36 and says why that is not the whole story.

**Both probes are now tests, not one-offs**: `npm run test:cold` runs
`test/coldvisitor.mjs` (iPhone 13 + Pixel 7) and `test/coldjourney.mjs`
(iPhone 13 + desktop). The visitor test asserts five things, three of which are
claims about the *offer* rather than the layout: every above-the-fold control
is 44px, nothing overflows sideways, the price is above the fold, what you get
for free is above the fold, and the "Make a book free" button is above the
fold. Those last three are one careless CSS change away from silently becoming
false, and nothing else in the repo would have noticed.

**A propagation lesson, learned the annoying way.** After the first deploy the
probe still reported 18px. The CSS was correct, the media query matched, the
computed style in a second browser said 45px. My `until curl ... grep -q`
propagation check had confirmed exactly one edge node; the browser had hit a
different one still serving the old HTML. Nothing was wrong except my
conclusion. Re-running the probe a minute later showed 45px.

Re-ran after both deploys: `test:mobile` (3 devices), `test:links` (44 pages,
167 links, 0 bad), `test:seo` (0 problems), `npm test` (90/90), `test:cold`
(4 runs). All green.

Live version is now `ff0a350d-882d-4ef5-9d76-60209ed0f9fe`. The rollback target

## Phase 25 — launch-scale failure modes, a font licence, and the answers

Nobody asked; the site was frozen; the useful question was what breaks or gets
asked tomorrow that I can settle tonight.

**Checked the two things a launch could actually break.** The rate limiter is
ten tries a minute per IP and sits only on `/api/verify` — page loads are static
assets with no limiter in front of them, so a Product Hunt flood cannot be
throttled off the site. And the unlock lookup already pages through Stripe
(`MAX_SCAN_PAGES = 20`, 100 a page, so 2,000 payments deep) with an honest
"ran out" message rather than telling a buyer they never paid. Past me did that
properly. Nothing to fix.

**The fonts turned up something real.** We embed Liberation Sans and serve the
two `.ttf` files publicly. Read the licence out of the fonts' own name tables
rather than assuming: version 2.1.5, SIL Open Font License 1.1, Red Hat and
Google copyright. That is clean for our customers — the OFL says in as many words
that the requirement for fonts to stay under it "does not apply to any document
created using the fonts", so a book with it embedded is unencumbered and owes
nobody a credit line.

But OFL condition 2 asks that redistributed font software carry the copyright
notice and the licence, and we were serving bare `.ttf`s. Small, real, and
exactly the thing an HN reader checks. Fetched the canonical text from the
upstream repo rather than typing a legal document from memory, and shipped it as
`public/fonts/LICENSE.txt`. Deployed `b8068dc0-2e93-4809-aa0b-641736ff99bf` —
wrangler reported "Uploaded 1 file (139 already uploaded)", which is the whole
audit: no code, no HTML, one new asset nothing links to. Verified live three
times over, home page and font unchanged. Rollback id in the runbook moved to
`d53de0d4`. Also narrowed the funnel's font counter to `.ttf` so a crawler
fetching a licence file never reads as somebody making a book.

**Then the thing that actually decides tomorrow: `marketing/answers.md`.** The
runbook had principles for answering on PH and HN but not a single sentence ready
to paste, and the bottleneck on a launch day is me drafting three paragraphs
while a thread goes cold. So fifteen questions answered now, while there is time
to check facts instead of recall them — is it AI, why $19, what stops me removing
the watermark, can I sell the books, the font licence, Book Bolt, sudoku
uniqueness, why browser-side, and the hostile one HN always asks: isn't KDP
already flooded with low-effort puzzle books, aren't you making it worse. That
one gets a real answer rather than a dodge, and ends with permission to remain
unconvinced.

Two claims did not survive being checked. "32 themes" did (and is test-covered).
"Letters come out around 23pt" did not: the grid auto-sizes off the page width,
and the code's own two comments say 22 and 23. Changed to "roughly 22–23pt" with
a note saying why a single number is a hostage.

**And the guard caught me again, which is twice on its first day.** `answers.md`
has "do not say we pad books to a page minimum" notes, which name the claim in
order to forbid it. The tempting fix was to exempt the file — wrong, because
`answers.md` is the one drafting file whose contents get pasted into public
threads, so it is precisely where the claim must never come back. Instead the
guard now skips a sentence that opens as an instruction to the writer ("do not
say", "never claim"). My first idea had been to skip *negated* sentences
generally, which would have been a disaster: the original falsehood was itself a
negation — "no padding to KDP's minimum", in a list of what other tools skip —

Re-proved it three ways: clean passes, the sentence in `index.html` fails it, and
a plain assertion of the claim in `answers.md` fails it too — so the prohibition
escape does not blanket-exempt the file. 91/91.
