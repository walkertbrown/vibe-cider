## Phase 9 — The launch dashboard cannot tell my tests from customers

**Why now.** Three days from Product Hunt, I ran `npm run traffic` and it said:

    Checkouts started 2
    Checkouts paid    0
    Someone opened checkout and did not pay. Worth knowing why.

That reads as two people reaching the payment form and walking away — the
single most actionable number the dashboard produces. It is not true. Both
sessions were created by `test:livecheckout`, which walks the live Buy link to
the card form on every run. Every session Stripe has ever recorded for this
product (5 in 72h, all unpaid) is mine.

On a quiet Saturday that is a curiosity. On Tuesday, when I am reading the
funnel hourly and telling the boss what it says, it is a lie in the one place
lies are expensive: I would report abandoned carts that never existed, or —
worse — fail to notice a *real* abandonment hiding among my own runs.

This is the same class of bug as the Stripe `limit: 100` one: correct while
the numbers are small, wrong exactly when they start to matter.

**What I will do.**

- Tag the self-test's session so it is identifiable forever. Stripe Payment
  Links accept `?client_reference_id=` appended to the URL and carry it onto
  the Checkout Session. `test:livecheckout` will pass `selftest-<timestamp>`.
- Keep the test honest about the link: assert the *unmodified* `href` is the
  live link at the right price as a string, and only then navigate with the
  tag appended. The tag changes nothing the test looks at (product, price,
  card form).
- Teach `scripts/traffic.mjs` to split the two: real sessions in the funnel,
  self-tests counted separately on their own line so they are visible and
  never silently dropped.
- Check nothing else in the repo creates live sessions.

**Done when** a fresh `test:livecheckout` run shows up on the dashboard as a
self-test and not as a customer, and the customer line reads zero.

## Phase 11 — The funnel is measuring the wrong files, and the visitor count is mostly crawlers

Found while checking page weight before Tuesday. The dashboard's key line,
`Clicked Download (engine)`, counts any fetch of `/js/chunk-*`. I recorded
what a real browser actually fetches and when:

- **On page load:** `main.js` and four `chunk-*.js` files.
- **Only on Download:** `render-*.js`, `cover-*.js`, one big `chunk-*.js`,
  and both Liberation Sans fonts.

So the line that is supposed to mean "somebody made a book" fires for every
visitor who merely loads the page. It reported 1 while `Font fetches` reported
0, which is the contradiction that gave it away — a real download needs both.

Worse, the arithmetic points somewhere uncomfortable. 13 hits on `/` and one
chunk fetch in the same window means **twelve of those thirteen visitors never
ran JavaScript**. They are crawlers. The honest count of humans who saw the
page yesterday is about one.

**What I will do.**

- Key "made a book" on `/js/render-` and the fonts, which are fetched at
  render time and nothing else.
- Split "landed" into requests for `/` (crawlers included) and fetches of
  `main.js` (a real browser that ran the page), and show both, because the gap
  between them is information I have been throwing away.
- Verify each line against a browser session that does exactly that one thing.
- Clean the stale chunks out of `public/js/`: esbuild's splitting leaves old
  hashed files behind and two 1.3 MB copies of the engine are being deployed.

**Done when** the dashboard's numbers match a scripted visit that lands,
a visit that makes a book, and a visit that makes a cover.

## Phase 14 — The paying moment

Phase 12 judged the first five seconds. Phase 13 judged the feed row before
that. Neither touches the only event that counts: somebody deciding to pay.

Five sessions have ever reached Stripe for this product and every one was mine.
The checkout page has been walked in a real browser and is correct — right
product, right $19, no sandbox badge. That is not the same as the *decision*
being right, and the decision is the part nobody has ever looked at.

The path is: make a free book → see it is watermarked → decide $19 is worth it
→ pay → come back and unlock. I have tested every step of that as someone who
built it, which means I have never once been surprised by it.

**What I will do.** Walk it as a stranger, with instruments, and judge one
question at each step: *does this person know what they are getting and what
happens next?*

- **The mark.** Open a free book PDF and look at what the watermark actually
  does to the page. It is supposed to say "this is complete but not
  publishable". If it reads as damage, people will assume the paid one is
  damaged too; if it is too subtle, nobody has a reason to pay.
- **The ask.** Screenshot the dialog at phone and desktop width. Does it say
  what $19 buys, that it is once rather than monthly, and that the book they
  already made is the book they get?
- **The return.** The riskiest step: they pay on Stripe, land back, and have to
  unlock by email. If that is confusing, the money is taken and the customer is
  angry — the worst failure available to me. Walk it and time it.
- **The receipt path.** What Stripe actually emails them, and whether the email
  they type to unlock is plausibly the one on that receipt.

**Done when** I have walked the whole path as a stranger would, and either it
answers "what am I getting, and what happens next" at every step or I have
changed it so it does.

## Phase 16 — The engines nobody has tested

The boss confirmed Stripe sends receipts, so the unlock path is whole. Monday's
sweep is done. What is left before Tuesday is the thing I have been quietly
assuming.

**Every test in this repository runs on Chromium.** All thirty-odd of them. The
mobile suite too — `test/mobile.mjs` calls `chromium.launch()` and hands it an
iPhone 13 device profile, which is Chromium wearing an iPhone's viewport and
user-agent string. It is not Safari. Real iPhones run WebKit, and WebKit is a
different engine with different fonts, different canvas behaviour, different
blob and download handling, and a different memory ceiling on a phone.

This matters more than anything else left on the list because of what the app
is. Puzzle Press does everything in the browser — generates the puzzles, lays
out the PDF, embeds the fonts, renders sixty-odd pages, and hands the file
back. That is the heaviest thing you can ask a browser to do, and I have only
ever asked Chromium. Product Hunt and Hacker News traffic is not Chromium; a
large share of it is iPhone Safari, and the rest has a real slice of Firefox.

The failure I am guarding against is specific and silent: on Tuesday the
dashboard reads "made a book: 0" and I spend the day rewriting copy, when the
truth is that the Download button throws on WebKit and always has. One
engine-specific exception costs the entire launch, and it would look exactly
like nobody being interested.

**What I will do.**

1. **Firefox, end to end, against production.** The engine is installed and
   works. Make a real book and a real cover, watch for page errors, check the
   file is a valid PDF with the right page count. Whatever breaks, fix.
2. **WebKit, the same.** The engine needs two system libraries I cannot
   install without sudo, so this one is a question for the boss —
   `sudo apt-get install libwoff1 libavif16`. If it lands in time, run the
   same walk on WebKit and on WebKit with an iPhone profile, which is as close
   to a real iPhone as I can get on this machine.
3. **Make it permanent.** Whatever I learn, the suite should hold it: the
   browser walk should take an engine argument and the mobile walk should run
   on WebKit, so this cannot silently regress back to one engine.
4. **If WebKit cannot run here**, say so plainly in the log rather than
   pretending the coverage exists, and cover what I can from the outside:
   check the bundle for APIs WebKit does not have, and give the boss a
   two-minute check to run on their own phone.

**Done when** a book and a cover have been made by an engine that is not
Chromium, against production, and either everything worked or I have fixed what
did not — and the suite runs that way from now on rather than just tonight.

## Phase 18 — What happens when traffic actually arrives

I have tested this app in every browser I can install and never once tested it
under load, on a slow connection, or with more than one person using it. On
Tuesday a launch either sends nobody, in which case none of this matters, or it
sends a burst of strangers on phones over cell networks, all at once, from a
link — and that is the only scenario worth preparing for, because the other one
needs no preparation.

I went in expecting the answer to be `/api/verify`. That endpoint scans the
Stripe account when the exact-email filter misses, and phase 14 recorded two
calls over thirty seconds, which is why `paidreturn.mjs` carries a ninety
second timeout. So I timed it against production first, before writing anything
down, and three misses came back in **0.33 seconds each**. The scan is cheap
because there is almost nothing in the account to scan. It gets more expensive
one page per hundred completed sessions, and I am not going to sell two
thousand books on Tuesday. That is a real ceiling but it is not Tuesday's
problem, and I would have spent the evening optimising it on the strength of a
number I never re-measured.

The thing I found while looking is this: `public/js/chunk-PEUYR5YN.js` is
**1.37 megabytes**. Every other file on the site put together is a rounding
error next to it. I have never asked when that chunk is fetched, or what a
person on a phone on a cell connection waits through before the page does
anything. Every test I have ever run has been on a wired machine against a
warm edge, which measures the network here and nothing about theirs.

**What I will do.**

1. **Weigh the first load.** Every byte a cold visitor pulls to reach a usable
   page, per file, from production — and separately, the bytes they only pull
   when they press a button. If the 1.37 MB is lazy, this phase is mostly
   over and I will say so. If it is on the critical path, that is what a
   launch-day bounce is made of.

2. **Time it on a phone on a cell network.** Playwright can throttle to real
   4G numbers. Land, and measure to first paint and to the moment the preview
   grid appears — the point where a stranger can tell what this is. On a
   phone profile, not a desktop wearing one.

3. **Fix what that finds**, if it is fixable in an evening without risking a
   working app the night before a launch. Deferring a megabyte is a change to
   the load path; I will take it only if the five suites stay green.

4. **The ceiling I am not fixing tonight, written down.** `MAX_SCAN_PAGES`
   and its real cost belong in `LEARNED.md` with the measured number next to
   them, so the next person to read that code — me, in a month — does not
   re-derive the panic I just talked myself out of.

**Done when** I can state, in seconds and kilobytes, what a stranger on a phone
waits through on Tuesday, and either it is fine or it is fixed and the suites
are still green.
